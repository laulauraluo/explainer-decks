Explainer · surfing

Every part of a surfboard has a job

The deck carries you, rails and fins grip the wave face, rocker keeps the nose from digging in, and the leash keeps the board near you after a fall.

1 · Swell

Wind makes the waves; the water mostly stays put

Wind blowing over open water passes energy into it, and that energy travels on as swell. The water near the surface mostly moves in small circles and ends up about where it started.

2 · Breaking

Waves break when the water gets too shallow for them

Over a rising bottom a wave slows, its crests bunch up and it grows steeper. A common rule of thumb: it breaks when its height reaches roughly 0.8 of the water depth.

3 · Reading a break

The bottom decides where a wave peaks and which way it peels

The white water shows the peel. Sandbars move, so beach breaks shift; a point bends the swell into one long peel; a reef makes the same shallow peak every time.

4 · Paddling out

Go under the white water, not through it

Paddle hard at the broken wave, push the nose down with straight arms, sink the tail with a knee, and let the turbulence pass over. The board's float brings you up behind it.

5 · Catching a wave

Match the wave's speed, then stand up in one move

Paddle toward the beach as the wave arrives. When it lifts your tail and the board starts to slide, push up, bring your feet under you and angle along the unbroken face.

6 · Lineup etiquette

The surfer closest to the peak has the wave

Whoever is closest to where the wave is breaking has priority. Taking off in front of them is a drop-in: let it go and wait for your own wave.

Takeaway

Read the swell, the bottom and the lineup before you go

  • Swell is moving energy; the water mostly circles in place.
  • Shallow water trips it: a wave breaks at roughly 0.8 of the depth.
  • The bottom sets the peak and the direction it peels.
  • Under, then up: duck dive the white water, pop up in one move.
  • Closest to the peak goes. Never drop in.
